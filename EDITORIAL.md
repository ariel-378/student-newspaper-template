# Editorial policy — a starting point

This is a template, not a policy. Copy it, argue about it with your adviser,
change what doesn't fit, and adopt the result. A policy nobody discussed is
worse than none, because it creates the impression of agreement where there
isn't any.

It exists because the software half of a student paper is the easy half. Every
question below will eventually be asked by an adviser, a subject of a story, or
next year's editor — usually at the worst possible moment. Answer them while
nothing is on fire.

> **Fill in every `[ ]` before adopting.** Where this template picks an option,
> it says so and gives the alternative.

---

## Who publishes

**This template assumes: student editors publish, and the adviser can pull
anything immediately.**

| Step | Who |
|---|---|
| Write and file | The writer |
| First edit — accuracy, fairness, clarity | The section editor |
| Second read and the decision to publish | `[ ]` Editor-in-Chief |
| Publish | `[ ]` Whoever holds publish access |

The adviser does not read every story first. In exchange, **the adviser can
unpublish anything, at any time, without asking or explaining first.** The
conversation happens afterwards.

**The alternative — prior review:** the adviser reads and approves every story
before it is visible. Slower, and it makes the adviser the publisher rather
than the safety net. Many schools require it, and at a private school it is
entirely the school's call. **Pick one and write it down.** The failure is
leaving it ambiguous until the first difficult story.

Whichever you choose, tell every editor with publish access on their first day.

### Does the online edition screen anything a second time?

**Decide this early, because it decides how much work the site is.**

Many papers put the same stories online that ran in print. If that is you, they
have already been reported, edited and approved once, and publishing online is
republishing rather than a second editorial decision. Say so — it answers the
question everyone asks first, which is *who checks this before it goes up.*

What still needs an answer either way is **anything with no print edition behind
it**: video, puzzles and games, and anything posted between issues as breaking
news. That material has not been through the print process, because there was no
print process for it. Whoever reads it first is a real decision, and it is the
adviser's to make.

### Who can pull something down

**This template assumes the adviser has the same access as the students** — the
same publish and unpublish rights, used directly, without asking anyone.

That is worth doing on purpose. An adviser who has to reach a teenager to get
something taken down does not really hold the authority everyone assumes they
hold, and the gap only shows up on the worst possible day. One group, one tier,
same rights.

### What gets a second adult read before publishing

Independence isn't recklessness. Regardless of which model you pick, bring
these to the adviser *before* publishing:

- A story accusing a **named** person of wrongdoing
- Discipline, investigations, or police matters
- A student's health, family, immigration status, sexuality — anything that is
  theirs to disclose and not the paper's
- Anything resting on an **anonymous source**
- Anything a subject has asked you not to run

The test isn't "are we allowed." It's *if this is wrong, who gets hurt, and can
it be undone?*

## Corrections

- **Typos and broken links** — fix silently.
- **Anything that changes what the story says** — append a dated correction
  saying what was wrong and what it now says. Describe the error; don't hide it.
- **Serious errors** — front page for one cycle.

Publish a correction address and answer every request, **including the ones you
decline**, saying why. The unanswered request is the failure, not the refused
one.

## Unpublishing

Default: **no.** A published story is part of the record.

Exceptions:

1. The adviser or school says so — immediate.
2. It is false and can't be fixed — remove, with a note saying why.
3. It endangers someone — safety outranks the archive.
4. A source who was a minor asks, the material is sensitive, and there's no
   public interest in the name. Prefer **unnaming** over unpublishing.

Not reasons: it's embarrassing now, they regret an on-the-record quote, a
college might see it. Say no kindly, and in writing.

### Deletion and version history

**This site's content lives in a git repository, and git keeps history.**
Removing a story from the live site removes it from what readers see. It does
**not** remove it from the repository's history.

If something must be *genuinely* gone, deleting the file is not enough — it has
to be purged from history and force-pushed. **Never tell a subject something
has been erased until that has actually happened.** If your repository is
public, assume anything ever committed is permanent.

## Bylines, anonymity, photographs

- Stories carry a byline. Taking your name with your work is most of what makes
  it journalism.
- A byline may be withheld if publishing it would put the writer at real risk —
  EIC and adviser both agree, and the story says so rather than pretending.
- Anonymous sources are a last resort, never a way to let someone attack a
  named person without accountability. The EIC must know the source. If the EIC
  can't know, it doesn't run.
- Photographs of students follow **the school's existing media-consent rules**.
  The paper doesn't get a looser standard than the yearbook.

## Reader data

The newsletter collects **an email address and nothing else** — no phone
numbers, by design. See `setup/README.md`.

- The subscriber list lives in a **school-owned** account, never a student's.
- Used only to send the paper.
- Anyone can leave by asking any editor, and someone must actually answer.

If you run shared editing, that service holds the paper's working content. On a
student's personal account, the paper loses it the day that account goes.

**Review every August.** An account nobody can get into is the ordinary way a
student publication loses its archive.

## Succession

The most likely way this site dies is that the people who set it up graduate.

- **Name a Web Editor from a younger class now**, with real publish access and
  stories actually published under supervision.
- **Put the role in the masthead** so it's filled every year, like Sports Editor.
- **Train each August**, on the masthead calendar, outgoing to incoming.
- **The adviser keeps independent access**, so a lost password is never the end
  of the paper.

Two people minimum understand how to publish, at all times. One is an outage
waiting for a bad week.

---

## When something goes wrong

1. **Take it down first** if anyone could be harmed. Argue afterwards. Nobody is
   ever in trouble for pulling something too fast.
2. **Tell the adviser the same day.**
3. **Write down what happened** while it's fresh.
4. **Correct in public** if readers saw it.

What ends student papers isn't publishing something wrong. It's the adviser
finding out from somebody else.
